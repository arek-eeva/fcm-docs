What it takes to run one inside a managed tenant.

What it is

A Chief of Staff is Claude Code, Anthropic's desktop application, working inside one folder on the user's own machine. There's no new platform to log into and no service of mine holding their data. It reads and writes real files in that folder, under their direction.

What makes it a Chief of Staff rather than a chat window is what's in the folder: a written operating brief for how the business runs, a memory of what's been decided and why, and a set of procedures it follows. Then it drafts, reconciles, chases, prepares and remembers, and it stops and asks before anything leaves the building.

The install itself is one application from Anthropic. That's the whole vendor surface to assess.

Where it stops being simple

Everything above runs cleanly for someone on their own machine with their own accounts. The moment the person sits inside a governed tenant I hit the same wall every time, and it's your side of the fence rather than mine:

  • App registration and Graph permissions
  • Conditional access
  • Device compliance
  • And underneath all three, how the thing authenticates at all when the organisation runs Microsoft SSO

How it connects, which is where my knowledge runs out

Three ways, depending on the tool. An API key held locally on the machine. A command-line connection. Or a browser login that establishes the session and hands back a token.

In a Google environment on a Mac those are close to effortless, which is most of what I've installed to date. Under Microsoft SSO I don't know which of the three survives, what the consent flow looks like from an administrator's side, or which of them you'd refuse outright and be right to.

Microsoft is also a suite rather than a product. Mail, calendars, storage, bookings, messaging. I can't tell you which of those have an interface an agent can reach cleanly and which don't. You can, and that alone would be worth the first conversation.

The two layers of security, which get conflated

The hard layer is yours. What that account can reach, where things are stored, how access is granted and revoked. That's tenant configuration and it's the layer I can't do and shouldn't pretend to.

The soft layer is mine. Rules written into the folder telling it what it may never do, what it must always do, and where it has to stop and ask before anything leaves the building. Access is granted one tool at a time rather than all at once, so it starts narrow and widens only where there's a reason.

The second layer is not a substitute for the first. It sits on top of it, and it's worthless if the first one is wrong.

The access problem, conceded first

The agent sees what the signed-in person can see. For most founders that's effectively everything, so scoping reduces the blast radius rather than removing it. That's the real risk, not where anything is hosted, and I'd rather say it than have you find it.

What the shape does buy is a smaller surface to argue about. A Chief of Staff is a personal layer for one senior person, not a company-wide deployment and not a seat for every employee. It runs top down, so nobody below reaches up through it. And access gets granted one capability at a time: read runs freely, anything that sends, spends, publishes or deletes stops and asks, and write is turned on deliberately rather than by omission.

Two things I won't dress up. Derived records are unsolved. Every summary and brief it writes is a new record holding extracted content, and it inherits the permissions of wherever it lands rather than of the source. Nothing alerts on that, by us or by anyone else I've read. The best I have is deciding up front where outputs go and applying the same controls there. And I never claim compliance. That depends on licensing, configuration, contracts and data class, none of which I control. I build to the controls the client's IT approves, which in this conversation would mean yours.

What I'm not asking you to do

You told me on Friday that you run chat only, with no agents and no desktop AI on any of your own devices, because you sit as a junction for every client's security information and one incident at your end reaches all of them.

That's the right posture and nothing here asks you to change it. This is about what a client of mine needs inside their own tenant, not about what runs inside yours.

The questions I have

You proposed the sensible version of this on Friday: bring you one live client with a blocker, you come in ad hoc, and we both find out whether this works. I'm taking that. I don't have that client today, and I'd rather tell you than invent one. These are the things I'd want answered by the time I do.

  • What does the enablement typically cost, and how long does it take?

    For a small business already on Microsoft 365, with nothing unusual about it.

  • Do you do that work for businesses that aren't already your managed clients?

    This is the one that decides whether the model works for me at all, so I'd rather have a straight no than a maybe.

  • Who does it, and how quickly could you turn one around?

    Less about capacity than about whether it's a named person I'd be working alongside.

And your question back to me, which I didn't answer on Friday and won't pretend is settled: who supports and maintains the back end once it's installed. The honest answer is that it depends on how much ends up sitting outside the folder, and that a first job together would tell us both more than either of us guessing now.
