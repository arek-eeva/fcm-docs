Your question · can it actually operate the platforms

You asked it three ways, and it's the same question each time: can it go in and do the work, or is that still my hands?

Short version: on the platforms you named, mostly yes, and the newsletter is the strongest case of the lot. Here it is platform by platform, with what it can't do stated as plainly as what it can.

Campaign MonitorYes, the whole loop
The best of your platforms for this. Its API covers creating a campaign, setting the content, sending yourself a test, scheduling it and sending it, then pulling opens, clicks and bounces back afterwards. Your templates already exist, so the monthly job is populating one: this month's articles into their slots, the link to the blog you've already published, the photos placed. That is exactly the shape of work an API does well.
MailchimpYes, with one caveat
The same capability is there. The caveat is worth knowing because it's the sort of thing you'd find out later: Mailchimp's own published connector only covers their transactional product, not the marketing side you use. The marketing API itself is mature and well documented, so the work gets done, it just gets done through a small piece we write rather than something Mailchimp hands over. More on that below, because it matters for your clients' data.
MetricoolYes, and it's official
Metricool publishes their own connector and it's hosted, so there's nothing to install. You authorise it in a browser once. It schedules posts, reads your analytics, tells you best posting times and pulls competitor data, in conversation, without opening the dashboard. One correction to what I said on the call: the connector works on the free account, but Metricool's full API is Advanced-plan only, and Advanced starts at about $53 a month for 15 brands, not 50. Fifty brands is a good deal more. I'd rather correct my own number than have you budget off it.
CanvaPartly, and the part that matters
Canva has a published connector too. It can generate designs, autofill your clients' brand templates, search, resize and export as PNG, PDF, MP4 or PowerPoint. What it cannot do is art-direct. But you weren't asking it to. You described the actual pain on the call: "I've got to get them out of Canva, download them, save them somewhere, upload them into" the newsletter. That loop is what dies. Template populated, exported, placed, without the download-rename-reupload in the middle.
The blog itselfYes, if it's WordPress
You publish to their sites before the newsletter goes out, so it's upstream of everything else. WordPress has an official adapter maintained by the WordPress project, and since March this year agents can draft, edit and publish posts and pages. New posts save as drafts by default and every action needs you to say yes, which is the same shape as the rest of this build rather than a bolt-on. Worth checking per practice, though: WordPress yes, a proprietary or agency-built site may have no way in at all.
The photosHalf, and it's the tedious half
Getting them out of the client is still you, because that's a human chasing a human. Everything after that isn't: renaming, resizing, cropping to the right ratio, placing them in the right slot, uploading. The anniversary and community photos you described are exactly the coordinator work you said isn't strategic. It isn't, and it shouldn't be yours.

The security answer your clients’ data, in full
First, the one you called critical
You hold accountants' data, and sometimes their clients'. It needs clear controls.

You said the security factor is critical for you, and you were right to put it first. You're not just carrying your own material. You're carrying eight or nine accounting practices' work, and inside some of it sits detail about their clients. Your exposure isn't embarrassment, it's a practice ringing you about confidentiality.

We only had time for a partial answer on the call. These are the three controls.

One. It isn't reading anything on its own. There's no standing process going through your mailbox or your client folders deciding what's interesting. It reads when you point it at one specific thing, the way you'd ask a person to go and find a thread. Point it at nothing and it sees nothing.

Two. Before a single thing is connected, we write down what it must never keep. Named clients, named categories, whole topics. Their clients' personal or financial details, anything a practice would treat as privileged, anything one of them has told you no about. That sits in the brief it reads before every task, and it governs the notes it writes, not only what it says back to you.

Three. We decide up front where its notes are allowed to land, and that place inherits the restrictions of wherever the material came from. The real failure in a setup like yours isn't the system reading a file. It's a summary appearing in a looser place than the source did. So we name the destination before there's anything to put in it.

And the part you should hear from me rather than find later: this is a design discipline, not a solved technical problem. Nothing in the industry reliably detects that a summary has picked up something sensitive. Anyone telling you that's handled is selling you something. What makes it safe is deciding the rules while the system is still empty, which is exactly why session one connects nothing at all.
