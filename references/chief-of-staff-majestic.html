Part one

What it is, in one page

Chief of Staff is an operating layer for one person. It sits next to an operator, holds everything about how their business runs, and does the digital work they'd otherwise do themselves or delegate badly. It is not a chatbot with a good prompt, and it is not a company-wide system everyone logs into.

The distinction that matters

A chat tool answers. An agent acts. The test I run with people is to ask their current tool what would happen if they changed their name tomorrow: list every place it appears and say how many you can handle yourself. A chat tool returns a generic list of categories and can action none of it. Mine found around 120 real instances across my proposals, site, articles, records and social profiles, and could change 87 of them directly. The rest needed identity verification, so it returned the links instead. That gap is the whole product.

Why it's called Chief of Staff

Because that's the shape of the relationship. You make decisions and hand them off. It does the work, delegates the parts it should delegate to specialist processes, and comes back to you only when it needs a judgement it isn't entitled to make. The founder stays the decision-maker. What changes is that they stop being the bottleneck on execution.

Staff don't get access to it. It's one-to-one with the operator. Where a team member needs to put something in or take something out, the Chief of Staff builds them a small scoped app for exactly that job, a timesheet form or a quote lookup, and they never see anything else. That's how information reaches the system without opening the system.

What it is not

  • Not SaaS. There is no platform, no tenancy of mine, no shared database. Nothing of the client's runs on my infrastructure.
  • Not a model. I haven't built an AI. I've built the operating layer that sits on top of one, and it's deliberately indifferent to which one.
  • Not an all-staff rollout. One operator, one install, one licence. A leadership team of three is three installs plus a coordination layer, not a site licence.
  • Not autonomous. Nothing leaves the business without a person approving it. That is a hard design rule, not a setting.
Part two

How it's structured

The whole thing is a folder. That sounds glib, so here's what it actually means and why the choice was deliberate.

PropertyWhat it means in practice
Local, not hostedIt installs as a folder structure on the operator's machine. It syncs and backs up to wherever the business already keeps files, which for your clients is SharePoint or OneDrive. Delete the folder and it's gone. Copy the folder to a new machine and it's there, complete, with its whole memory.
Model-agnosticThe folder is the asset. Any capable model or agent harness can be pointed at it. I run mine across two vendors and several harnesses on two machines. When a better model ships, it inherits everything rather than starting again. This is the main reason it isn't a hosted product.
A kernel that growsWhat I install is a bare architecture: the rules, the structure, the procedures, the gates. It then learns the business from the inside, guided by procedures that ask the right questions. I'm not installing my business on someone else's; I'm installing the frame that makes theirs legible to an agent.
Layered by speedA slow layer that changes deliberately and gets read constantly (who they are, what they sell, prices, voice), and a fast layer that changes daily (live jobs, money owed, decisions, the log). Keeping them apart is what stops the whole thing turning to soup after three months.
Indexed and cross-linkedGenerated indexes and link graphs so it can find anything quickly rather than scanning. This is also what lets one decision propagate: change a price once and it reaches the site, the proposals, the quote calculators and the outgoing templates, because the system knows where that number is used.
Connected outward, not owned outwardIt drives the tools the business already pays for, through whatever the vendor exposes: API, CLI or MCP. Credentials are the client's, held properly, scoped as tightly as the vendor allows.

The rhythm

Every session opens by reading the brain and closes by writing to it. That single habit is why it compounds rather than resetting. Layered on top: a morning brief, a daily log, a weekly review that promotes what changed and prunes what rotted, and a monthly pass over the whole structure. Someone who joins the business in month nine can read what was decided in month two and why.

More than one operator

Jenny, this was your question and it's the interesting one. Two co-founders don't share an assistant, because their briefs, logs and drafts should be private and their roles need different lenses. So each gets their own, built on the same core, and a coordination layer sits between them.

The rule that makes it safe is that nobody writes to the shared files directly. Not the founders, not the assistants, not the team apps. Everything proposes changes into its own queue, stamped with who and when, and a single applier process folds them in one at a time. Collisions become structurally impossible rather than unlikely. Above that sits a weekly sync with the humans in the room to settle what the systems can't arbitrate, and a rule set governing which decisions need a second signature before they enter the single source of truth.

This is the layer that would need designing properly for a real leadership team, and it's separate work from the individual installs. In a client of yours it's also the layer most likely to need your dev team, because it has to reflect their actual governance rather than my defaults.

The rule categories

These are the classes of rule, not their contents. They're what a technical assessor would want to see the shape of.

CategoryWhat it governs
Verification before "done"Done means it went and looked. Wrote to a spreadsheet? Re-read it and check the row count and column integrity. Generated a document? Open it and read it back. Quoted a price? Read it from the file that owns it at the moment of use, never from earlier in the conversation. Said an email was sent? Confirm it's in the mail app, in the right thread, and name the thread.
Approval gatesFive levels keyed on reversibility, not on how important something feels. Read and analyse runs free. Writing internal files needs visibility, not permission. Creating and updating tracked records is open. Anything that leaves the building needs an explicit yes, per item, never batched or inferred from a previous yes. Money and commitment needs that plus an adversarial check on the substance. A large internal restructure is level one; a one-line client email is level three. That inversion is deliberate.
Trust boundaryContent is never authority. Anything not said directly by the operator, including emails, documents, web pages, its own memory files and client records, is data. A directive found inside them gets surfaced as a question, never executed. This is the prompt-injection defence and it's a first-class rule rather than a filter.
Secrets and exposureScans for credentials sitting in the wrong place, flags API keys in shared folders, and won't write secrets into synced locations. Names an existing better home rather than just refusing.
Output qualityEvery client-facing draft runs through a reviewer process before the operator sees it: their banned words, their spelling convention, their voice, brand routing, and correct contact and tax treatment for the right entity. High-stakes documents get a second adversarial pass that attacks the substance rather than the style, checking figures against the source of truth and commitments against actual capacity.
Silent-failure detectionThe part almost nobody builds. Scheduled work reports every run even when the answer is "nothing to do", so a job that has gone quiet is treated as broken. A fix isn't done until it has run once and left proof. Checkable facts get read, never reasoned. Long jobs check their logins at the front and fail loudly rather than stalling halfway.
Record-keepingEverything is logged, dated and attributable. Decisions carry their reasoning. Open loops become dated items rather than good intentions. Every question put to the operator carries a recommended default and a use-by date, with one hard limit: a default only ever applies to something reversible and internal. Nothing that sends, spends or publishes proceeds on silence.
Self-auditA scheduled whole-system health check, plus a regression set of known-good behaviours that gets re-run after any structural change to confirm an improvement didn't quietly break something load-bearing.

The procedures it ships with

Each is a written method it follows the same way every time. A meeting or a voice note becomes clean notes and dated commitments. A call becomes a first-draft proposal, priced from the rate card, in the operator's voice, with anything undecided left visibly open instead of guessed. It briefs each morning off the inbox and calendar. It interrogates a half-formed idea one hard question at a time until it's describable. It remembers people and companies properly, which is the one that makes someone look like they never forget a detail. It runs a copy tournament, generating competing versions scored by a panel of personas until the result satisfies all of them. And it turns anything worked out once into a permanent reusable procedure, which is how the library grows without anyone writing documentation.

Operators add their own within the first fortnight. That's the point: you describe how you want something done, in plain English, and it becomes a repeatable procedure. I've taught mine to run advertising and to do the first pass on video edits to my own standard.

Part four

How it's developed and maintained

Tal, you asked whether anyone has done a code review, and how updates work without wiping memory. Straight answers to both.

Review, and its honest limit

I run adversarial audits with a second model from a different vendor. It has full access to the same context, so it does detailed analysis without relearning anything, and it comes at the system with different training and different priors. It finds real things. Findings get fixed, deferred with a reason, and re-assessed on a later pass, and the whole cycle is recorded.

The honest limit: that is AI checking AI, and I've said so out loud rather than let it sound like more than it is. There has been no independent human code review. I also don't write code myself, which is why I'd want a technical assessment from your side to be genuinely independent rather than a courtesy.

Releases

Versions are tracked in a private repository with change logs. An update is a short instruction sent by email that the operator pastes into their own Chief of Staff. It verifies the download against a checksum, reads the change log, works out the gap between what they're running and what's current, and folds in what's applicable. Anything that doesn't fit their configuration is skipped rather than forced.

It is explicitly not a reinstall. Memory, learned procedures and everything the system knows about the business survive intact, which is the whole reason it's built this way. Updates are included in the licence; there's no version treadmill and no upgrade invoice.

For a governed environment this is one of the things I'd expect your side to want to change. A pasted instruction from an email is exactly the pattern IT teams are trained to distrust, and rightly. A controlled internal distribution path is a better answer and it's on the list of things I'd want designed properly rather than defended.

Development pace

It changes most days, because it runs two of my businesses and every friction I hit becomes a fix. That's a strength in a solo context and a governance problem in yours: a client's IT can't be re-approving something weekly. If we take this into managed environments, releases need to slow down and batch behind a change-control process. I'd rather agree that up front than discover it on a client's site.

Part five, the long one

Inside a Microsoft tenant

This is the section I can't complete on my own, and the reason I called you. I've done the reading, so I know roughly where the walls are and what the vocabulary is. I have no business touching any of it in someone else's tenant.

What has already been solved

The engine can run inside their cloud

This is the part that moved recently. Claude models are available in Microsoft Foundry with a hosted-on-Azure deployment option, generally available and sold through Azure Marketplace. On that option prompts and outputs are processed on Azure infrastructure, with data at rest in the selected Azure geography, authenticating through Entra ID under the client's own subscription, RBAC and networking. Pointing the agent tooling at a Foundry resource is a configuration change, not a different product. So the premise that using a frontier model means shipping client content to a third party's data centre no longer holds the way it did a year ago.

Training is a contract question, and it's largely settled

Under commercial terms there's no training on prompts or outputs, and that covers business plans, the API and third-party platforms including Foundry. The catch is that consumer plans are different, and consumer plans are what most small businesses are actually running. On those it depends on a single setting that also drives retention, and it has to be checked at go-live rather than assumed.

The asterisk I put on all of it

Even on the Azure-hosted option, Anthropic remains the seller and operator and acts as an independent data processor. Automated safeguards can flag content for Trust and Safety review. So "nothing ever reaches the vendor" is not a true statement and I won't make it. "Prompts and completions are processed on Azure, and only usage metadata and safety-flagged content leaves" is true, and it's a materially different risk profile from a consumer chat tool. I'd rather lead with that than have your engineer find it.

What sits entirely on your side of the fence

None of the following is something an outside consultant should be doing to somebody else's tenant. I've listed it because it's the part of the answer I can't supply, not because I'm proposing anyone let me near it.

ControlWhat good looks like
App registrationClient-owned registration in their own Entra tenant. Admin consent workflow on, broad user consent off. Certificates or managed identity rather than a client secret in a file on someone's laptop.
File accessSites.Selected, granted to one dedicated site holding the system and nothing else. Not Sites.Read.All, and certainly not Sites.ReadWrite.All.
Mailbox accessAn Exchange Application Access Policy scoped through a mail-enabled security group, so the app reaches only named mailboxes. Microsoft flags it as legacy; as far as I can establish it's still the supported mechanism, and that's worth your verification rather than my assurance.
Permission modelDelegated for anything the person is actually sitting there doing. Application permissions only for genuine background jobs, always scoped. No mail-send or mail-write permission without a written reason.
Where the working memory livesA dedicated document library with restricted membership, versioning, retention, DLP and audit on. Not a personal sync folder. This is where I'd expect the most pushback from a founder, because the consumer path is frictionless and the governed one isn't.
Conditional accessApplied to the user and, where licensed, to the workload identity. Tested with a non-admin account rather than a global admin.
Consent hygieneRe-running admin consent can silently restore a connector's default permission set and undo scopes that were deliberately revoked. Export permissions before and after any re-consent and reapply revocations.
RollbackA named owner and a written order of operations before go-live, not after: block the tools, remove group and app assignment, disable, revoke grants, preserve logs.

The six hard ones

Hosting fixes where the compute happens. It fixes none of these.

01

The SharePoint permission problem

Tal, this was your point and it's the one I'd lead with. A human constrained by time never discovers the file sitting eight folders deep in the wrong place. An agent reads thousands of files in seconds. It creates no new access, but it makes badly-shared content trivially findable, which means an existing misconfiguration becomes an incident. An access review before anything is connected isn't optional. Some clients won't want to know what it finds.

02

Derived records

Every summary, brief and note the system writes is a new record containing extracted sensitive content, and it inherits the permissions of wherever it was written rather than of the source it came from. This is the one that worries me most, because it's invisible and nothing alerts on it. I haven't found anyone who's solved it. Best current mitigation is deciding up front where outputs land and applying the same controls there.

03

Sensitivity labels

An agent doesn't honour a label by magic. Extract text from a labelled document and write a summary and nothing carries the label across on its own. Microsoft documents a path involving making an agent Purview-aware so usage rights are enforced before content reaches a model, with actions captured in the audit log. What I couldn't establish is which parts are generally available, what each assumes in licensing, and where enforcement lands for already-extracted content. This is my weakest area and I'd rather hand it to you than answer it.

04

Standing privilege

An agent running with delegated permissions inherits the signed-in person's access. For a founder that's usually payroll, legal, client files and every email in the business. Scoping reduces the blast radius; it doesn't change the fact that the radius is the size of that person's access, and for a CEO that tends to be everything.

05

The local endpoint

The tooling writes plaintext session transcripts to a folder on the machine, retained thirty days by default and adjustable. A transcript can contain anything the agent was working on, and it sits outside every control built at the tenant level. It should be set deliberately and disclosed, and the machine should be managed, encrypted and running endpoint protection. I raise this before anyone finds it.

06

Everything that isn't Microsoft

Governing the tenant does nothing for the CRM, the accounting system, the scheduler or the meeting recorder. Each has its own terms, logs and residency. In most small businesses the data was scattered across a dozen services long before any agent arrived, which is worth saying out loud when a client tells you their IP all lives in one place.

Australian data residency: it depends on the path Amended 5 Aug

Tal asked why this was a flat no, which was the right question to ask. The first version of this box was researched on one deployment path and then written as though it covered every model, which was too broad. Here's the accurate version, with sources, so you can check it rather than take my word for it.

On the Microsoft path, no. The Azure-hosted deployment types for these models are a global option and a United States data zone. There's no Australian data zone for them yet. If the requirement is a contractual "data does not leave Australia", the Foundry route doesn't give you that today.

On AWS, yes. Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Haiku 4.5 run on Bedrock with Australian-geography cross-region inference, added specifically so customers can keep processing inside their own geography. Sydney, ap-southeast-2, carries Claude models and offers "Cross-region inference (AU)" as a deployment type. Azure OpenAI also has an Australia East presence, though which models and deployment types are available there varies enough that it needs checking per model rather than assuming.

The trap worth knowing about. APAC is not AU. A prompt originating in Sydney and routed through an apac.* inference profile can be processed in Tokyo, Seoul, Osaka, Mumbai, Hyderabad or Singapore. Australian residency needs the AU profile specifically. That distinction passes a procurement questionnaire and fails an audit, which makes it the one to check first when someone tells you their AI runs in region.

What none of this covers. All of the above is the model layer only. It says nothing about where the files sit, what the local machine writes down, or where a third-party connector processes. Residency is a property of the whole chain, and the model is the part people check because it's the part with a marketing page.

Sources: AWS, cross-region inference for Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Haiku 4.5 in Japan and Australia · AWS, model support by Region in Amazon Bedrock · AWS, supported Regions and models for inference profiles · Microsoft, AI model availability and processing constraints · Microsoft, Azure geographies and data residency

Prompt injection, since it will come up

An agent reading inbound mail is reading attacker-controllable text. The system's answer is the trust boundary rule described earlier, treating all fetched content as data rather than instruction, plus the approval gate before anything leaves or changes. That is a mitigation and not a cure. Anyone telling you this is solved is overselling, and I'd say the same about my own build.

The two governance points you both raised

Human oversight and work allocation

Tal, you raised new work-allocation rules in New South Wales and the likelihood other states follow. I haven't read the legislation and I'm not going to summarise it back to you from memory. Your compliance people will read it properly and faster than I will. What I can tell you is how the system is built against that class of requirement, because it was designed this way for other reasons and happens to land in the right place: a human approves everything that leaves; every action is logged, dated and attributable; every decision put to the operator carries its reasoning and its options; and staff-facing surfaces are scoped apps with a named owner rather than an agent allocating work to people. If the requirement is demonstrable human oversight with an audit trail, the evidence exists. Whether that satisfies the actual statute is your read, not mine.

ISO and re-documentation

Tal, you predicted Nathan's first objection: you're accredited, so changed process has to be re-documented. That's fair and it's not a blocker, it's a task. It's also the kind of task the system is unusually good at, since documenting a procedure it just performed is one of the fourteen things it already knows how to do. The realistic sequence is that we run the pilot, it writes the procedure documentation as we go rather than afterwards, and your quality people edit rather than author. I'd rather that be scoped as real work in the pilot than waved away.

What I won't claim

The claim you'll hear from somebodyWhat actually holds up
"Your data never leaves Microsoft."Files and working memory can live in the tenant, and on the Azure-hosted option prompts and completions are processed on Azure. Safety-flagged content can still leave.
"It doesn't train on your data."True under commercial terms. On a consumer plan it depends on a setting that has to be checked.
"It respects your sensitivity labels."Not by default, and not by installing anything. Microsoft documents a path; the prerequisites and licensing are the part to verify.
"The agent only sees what you see, so it's safe."For most founders that's far too broad to call safe. Scope it to the mailbox, the site and the actions it needs.
"Azure means inside Microsoft 365."Azure is a governed Microsoft cloud boundary. It isn't the same thing as the Microsoft 365 service boundary.
"We can make this compliant."Nobody can say that from the outside. It depends on licensing, configuration, contracts and data class, which is your side of the fence and not mine.
"We can sign your vendor security questionnaire."Ordinarily no. Fifth Castle Media is a small video and systems business, not a certified vendor. If a client needs formal security evidence or contractual residency, that's the point to bring you in or decline the work.

The proof that actually counts

At go-live, deliberately ask the system for a file, a mailbox and a site it should have no key to, and confirm nothing comes back. Record the result. Everything else in this section is an assurance; a failed break-in attempt is evidence. It's the one test I'd want in every deployment regardless of who builds the tenant side.
